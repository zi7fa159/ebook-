# Prologue: The Concrete Womb

The transport smelled of stale sweat, unwashed bodies, and the faint, metallic tang of fear. D-9341 pressed his forehead against the vibrating metal wall, the cold a welcome distraction from the nauseating lurch of the vehicle. He didn’t know where he was, only that it was far from any semblance of the life he’d known – a life that had officially ended the moment the judge’s gavel fell, condemning Marcus “Mars” Cole to death, then commuting it to… this. Whatever *this* was.

They’d been on the move for days, it felt like. Shuffled between windowless trucks, cargo planes that sounded like they were tearing themselves apart in mid-air, and now this rumbling beast that seemed to be descending deep into the earth, or perhaps just a very remote, very unpleasant part of it. His fellow passengers, a motley crew of similarly orange-clad individuals, were a mix of stoic dread and barely suppressed panic. No one spoke. The guards, clad in black tactical gear with visors that reflected no light, offered no comfort, only silent, imposing menace.

A final, bone-jarring halt threw them forward. The rear doors of the transport hissed open, revealing not daylight, but the sterile, unforgiving glare of industrial floodlights. A blast of frigid air, carrying the scent of pine, diesel, and something else… something faintly ozonic and unsettling, washed over them.

“Out! Single file! Move it, scum!” The voice was harsh, amplified, devoid of any human warmth.

D-9341 shuffled forward with the others, boots scuffing on a concrete ramp that led down into a vast, subterranean hangar. The scale of the place was staggering. Massive blast doors lined one wall, vehicles that looked more like military hardware than civilian transports were parked in neat rows, and everywhere, figures in similar black uniforms, or stark white lab coats, moved with purpose. This wasn't a prison. It was something else. Something colder.

They were herded into a processing area, a sterile white room that reeked of disinfectant. One by one, they were subjected to a brutally efficient series of medical checks, biometric scans, and what felt like an interrogation disguised as a psychological evaluation. Questions about fears, phobias, recurring nightmares, pain thresholds. D-9341 answered with the practiced apathy of a man who had learned long ago that revealing any vulnerability was a mistake.

Finally, a man in a crisp, grey suit, his face an unreadable mask, addressed them. His name tag read ‘Deputy Director Thorne’. His voice was calm, almost gentle, which somehow made it more menacing than the guards’ barked commands.

“Welcome to Site-113,” he began, his eyes sweeping over their haggard faces. “You are here because the world outside has deemed you… expendable. Your past lives, your crimes, your names – they are irrelevant now. Here, you are D-Class personnel. Your designation is your identity. Your purpose is to serve the Foundation.”

He paused, letting the words sink in. “The Foundation is dedicated to the security and containment of anomalous objects, entities, and phenomena. Objects that defy natural law, entities that threaten humanity, phenomena that could unravel reality itself. Your role in this is simple: you will assist in research and containment procedures. You will do what is asked of you, without question, without hesitation.”

A cold dread, colder than the Alaskan air outside, began to seep into D-9341’s bones. Anomalous objects? Entities? This sounded like something out of a madman’s fever dream.

“Many of the tasks you will perform are… hazardous,” Thorne continued, his expression unchanging. “But understand this: your service here, however brief or however long, contributes to a cause far greater than any individual life. Compliance ensures a smoother process for everyone. Defiance…” He let the word hang, a silent, potent threat. “Defiance will be met with immediate and severe consequences.”

They were issued new jumpsuits, these ones with their designations stenciled across the back. D-9341. No longer Marcus Cole. He was a number, a piece of equipment.

Led through endless corridors of bare concrete and steel, the air thrumming with a low, almost sub-audible hum, they arrived at a block of cells. Small, featureless, each with a thin mattress, a toilet, and a sink. Home.

As the heavy door to his cell clanged shut, sealing him in the sterile silence, D-9341 stared at his reflection in the polished steel of the sink. The man looking back was a ghost, stripped bare. But beneath the fear, a flicker of the old Mars remained – the survivor, the pragmat tốc.

He didn’t know what horrors this “Foundation” held, what “anomalies” he would face. But one thing was certain: D-9341 would not go gentle into this concrete womb. He would learn. He would adapt. He would survive.

Or he would die trying. The alternative, passive acceptance, was not an option. Not yet. Not while he still drew breath. The air here might be cold and carry the scent of unknown terrors, but it was still air. And he was still alive. For now.
