# Chapter 1: The Glitch in the Machine

One week later, the memory of the encounter still clung to Alex like the cloying scent of ozone after a lightning strike. He’d tried to rationalize it, dissect it with the cold logic he applied to debugging stubborn code. He’d scoured forums, wikis, even delved into the murky depths of Reddit threads that hadn’t seen a new post in years. He found countless stories, theories, and supposed "proofs," but nothing that definitively explained his experience. The server remained stubbornly absent from any list he could find. It was as if it had been a collective hallucination, a digital phantom.

He threw himself into his freelance coding work, hoping the familiar embrace of Python scripts and database management would dispel the unease. It worked, for a while. The mundane rhythm of deadlines and client meetings was a comforting antidote to the surreal. He even managed to convince himself it had been an elaborate prank, a well-orchestrated bit of theatre by some bored, technically proficient individuals.

The first new anomaly was subtle, almost dismissible.

Alex was working on a complex algorithm for a logistics company, optimizing delivery routes. He had multiple instances of his preferred code editor open, along with several browser tabs for research and documentation. His main development environment was a heavily customized Minecraft-themed IDE skin he’d built for fun – blocky textures, pixelated fonts. It was his personal touch, a nod to the game that had first sparked his interest in programming.

He was compiling a particularly dense module when his secondary monitor, usually displaying system resource usage and a music player, flickered. For a split second, the familiar green and brown of a Minecraft landscape flashed across the screen – not a screenshot, but what looked like a live, first-person view from within the game. It was gone before he could fully register it, replaced by his usual desktop.

Alex blinked, rubbing his eyes. "Too much caffeine," he muttered, dismissing it as a driver hiccup or a momentary graphical glitch. His system was powerful, but not infallible.

Later that day, it happened again. This time, he was on a video call with a client, discussing database schema. The client’s face suddenly pixelated, not in the usual way of a poor connection, but as if it were being rendered by Minecraft’s chunky graphics engine. The effect lasted only a second or two, then resolved. The client, a portly man named Henderson, didn’t seem to notice.

"So, for the user authentication table," Henderson continued, oblivious, "we’ll need to ensure hashing is implemented for all password fields."

"Right," Alex said, his voice a little strained. "Hashing. Of course." He made a note, but his mind was racing. Two visual anomalies in one day, both eerily reminiscent of Minecraft. Coincidence?

That evening, trying to unwind, he launched a modern version of Minecraft, his own private, heavily modded server he shared with a few old friends. He needed the comfort of the familiar, the controlled environment where he was the admin, the master of the domain. He flew his character over vast, procedurally generated landscapes, the vibrant colors and complex structures a far cry from the stark simplicity of the Alpha server.

He was building an extension to his already elaborate mountaintop castle when he noticed something odd. A row of trees at the edge of a nearby forest biome. Their leaves were missing. Just bare, skeletal branches reaching towards the sky. This was a known, albeit rare, glitch in some versions, but not one he’d encountered with his current mod-pack. He flew closer to investigate. As he approached, the game stuttered, lagging for a moment before the leafless trees abruptly vanished, replaced by fully foliated, perfectly normal oaks and birches.

Alex landed his character, a growing sense of unease prickling his skin. He walked through the forest, examining the trees. Nothing seemed out of place. Had he imagined it?

He decided to check the server logs. As an admin, he had access to every command, every player action, every system message. He alt-tabbed to his server console, scrolling through the recent entries. Player connections, chat messages, routine tick reports. Nothing unusual. He was about to close the console when a single line, buried amongst the system spam, caught his eye. It was in a different color, a dark, ominous red that wasn't part of the standard console output.

`[UNKNOWN] Herobrine entity data updated.`

Alex’s blood ran cold. `[UNKNOWN]`? There was no user or system process that should generate such a message. And "Herobrine entity data"? His server had no mods related to Herobrine. He’d specifically avoided them, finding the whole mythos more annoying than intriguing until his recent encounter.

He frantically typed commands, trying to query any entity named "Herobrine." The server returned nothing. He searched for the log message source. Untraceable. It was as if the message itself was a ghost in the machine.

He shut down the server, then his computer. The apartment felt too quiet, the glow of his modem’s LEDs like watchful eyes in the dark. He paced the small living room, the rational part of his brain battling a rising tide of panic.

This wasn't just a game anymore. The glitches, the log message – they were seeping out, a digital infection spreading through his systems. The line from the Alpha server echoed in his mind: `You shouldn't have come looking.`

He had dismissed it as a prank. But what if the prank wasn't confined to an obscure, forgotten server? What if the entity, the legend, the *thing* known as Herobrine, was more than just code? What if it was an idea, a virus, capable of traversing the digital ether, leaving its mark wherever it pleased?

Alex thought of Ben, an old online acquaintance who ran a moderately successful blog dedicated to paranormal phenomena, with a particular focus on digital hauntings and creepypastas. Ben "CryptoCurious" Carter. Alex had always found Ben’s enthusiastic belief in the bizarre to be amusing, a harmless eccentricity. Now, he found himself desperately wanting to talk to someone who wouldn’t immediately dismiss his experiences as stress-induced hallucinations.

He found Ben’s contact details and typed out a message, his fingers hesitating over the send button. It sounded insane, even to him. *My computer is haunted by a Minecraft ghost.*

Before he could send it, his main monitor, the one with his custom Minecraft IDE skin, flickered again. This time, it wasn’t a fleeting image. The screen went black for a moment, then resolved into a perfect, pixel-for-pixel recreation of a Minecraft crafting table interface. But instead of the usual grid and item slots, there was a single item in the center output slot: a block of bedrock, an item unobtainable in survival mode, unbreakable.

And etched into its surface, in jagged, pixelated letters, was a single word:

`HELLO.`

Alex stumbled back from his desk, a strangled cry escaping his lips. The image remained on the screen for a full ten seconds, then his normal desktop wallpaper reappeared as if nothing had happened.

He didn't need to send the message to Ben anymore. He needed to call him. Now. This wasn't just a glitch in the machine. The machine was trying to communicate. And it knew his name. Well, not his name, but it knew he was there. It was addressing *him*.

The digital ghost was no longer content to haunt forgotten servers. It was knocking on his door.
