# Chapter 8: The Price of Whispers

The idea of proactively seeking out "Null" was a line Alex hadn't anticipated crossing. His interactions so far had been reactive – the entity manifesting, him and Eve analyzing. But Eve was right; they were working with a dataset of one, maybe two if they counted her historical knowledge. If Herobrine – or Project Brother, as Eve insisted on its more accurate, chilling designation – was indeed spreading, or attempting to, they needed a broader view.

“Null operates on a principle of exchange,” Eve typed into their secure chat. They were discussing the logistics, the risks. Alex was back in his apartment, the new hardened laptop his only window to Eve and their grim research. He’d taken to sleeping with a baseball bat beside his bed, a primitive defense against an enemy that could rewrite reality with code. “They don’t offer services for free. Information has a price.”

“And we don’t exactly have a budget for black-hat hackers,” Alex replied, a wry twist to his lips. “What are we offering? Lifetime subscriptions to my non-existent stream?”

“Null isn’t always interested in monetary payment,” Eve countered. “Sometimes it’s data they can’t otherwise access. Sometimes it’s a specific, rare piece of code. Sometimes it’s a… service. Anonymity is their currency, and access is their commodity.”

Alex felt a prickle of unease. “What kind of service?”

“Let’s not speculate. First, we need to make contact. And we need to have a clear request. ‘Is anyone else seeing this Herobrine thing?’ is too vague. We need specifics. Are there other servers showing the ‘marker’ signature we found? Are there unusual traffic patterns between known game server clusters and, say, Mojang’s authentication servers that might indicate a coordinated spread or attack?”

Crafting the request for Null took them the better part of a day. It had to be concise, technically precise, and alluring enough to pique the interest of someone who likely dealt in state secrets and corporate espionage as a matter of course. They focused on the unique hexadecimal marker, its potential link to adaptive AI, and the possibility of a novel, self-propagating digital entity emerging within a global gaming network. They hinted at possessing unique telemetry from a primary infection point (Alex’s Kilo-Core) without revealing too much.

Eve had an old, heavily encrypted contact method for Null, one she admitted she hadn’t used in years, from a time when she was desperately trying to find anyone who would listen to her warnings about Mojang’s buried projects. “No guarantee this channel is still monitored by Null, or even by the same Null,” she cautioned. “Identities in that world are fluid.”

She sent the message. The wait began.

This time, it was shorter. Less than twenty-four hours later, a reply appeared in Eve’s XMPP client, automatically forwarded to Alex. It wasn’t from the same address Eve had used. This was a new, anonymous XMPP ID, its profile blank. The message itself was a single line:

`> Query: Interesting. Corroborating data required. Specify desired exchange.`

Null. Or at least, *a* Null. They were listening.

“They’re cautious,” Alex observed. “They want to see some of our cards before they show theirs.”

“Standard procedure for them,” Eve replied. “We need to offer something valuable enough to demonstrate credibility, but not so much that it compromises our core findings or our security.”

They decided to share a sanitized, heavily annotated log file from Kilo-Core, showing the initial appearance of the hexadecimal marker, its correlation with specific, advanced anomalous behaviors, and cross-references to some of Eve’s (decades-old but still relevant) theories on the “Brother” code’s potential for self-modification. They emphasized the unique nature of the telemetry, data no one else would have.

For the “desired exchange,” they kept it focused: “Confirmation of identical or variant marker signatures on other publicly accessible Minecraft servers (Java Edition, latest stable build). Associated anomalous network traffic patterns, if any. Raw data preferred.”

They sent it off. The reply came within hours.

`> Data packet received. Analysis pending. Proposed exchange: We provide list of up to five (5) server IPs exhibiting strong correlation with your marker signature, plus raw packet captures (up to 1GB total) from those nodes over a 7-day period. In return: Full, unredacted client-side and server-side logs from your ‘primary infection point’ for the 48-hour window surrounding the first confirmed marker appearance. Plus, any and all data pertaining to the ‘Brother’ project you possess.`

Alex felt a chill. “They want everything. All of Kilo-Core’s logs around that time, and all your Project Brother research, Eve.”

Eve was silent for a long moment. Alex could almost feel her weighing the options through the digital ether. Her research was her life’s work, the secret she’d guarded for over a decade, the source of her professional ruin and her personal obsession.

“The Kilo-Core logs are a risk,” Eve finally typed. “They could potentially identify your server’s original IP, your personal details if there’s any metadata we missed. But the ‘Brother’ data… that’s the core of it. That’s what they’re really after.”

“Why?” Alex asked. “Why would Null care so much about some ancient Mojang R&D project?”

“Knowledge is power, Alex. Especially knowledge others have tried to suppress. The ‘Brother’ code, if it was as advanced as I remember, could be a blueprint for… many things. Weaponized AI, next-generation malware, corporate espionage tools. Or perhaps Null has a more academic interest. Or a personal one. We’ll likely never know their true motives.”

“So, do we do it?”

“The potential gain is significant,” Eve mused. “If Herobrine is spreading, those server IPs and packet captures could give us the early warnings we need, help us map its trajectory. It’s a devil’s bargain. We trade our deepest secrets for a glimpse of the wider battlefield.” She paused. “But if we do nothing, we remain blind. And this thing is not going to wait for us to catch up.”

The decision hung heavy between them. The price of these whispers from the digital underground was steep.

“Okay,” Alex said finally, his voice firm. “Let’s do it. But Eve, your ‘Brother’ data – are you sure? After all this time?”

“Some secrets are too dangerous to keep, Alex,” Eve typed, a hint of weariness in her words. “Especially if keeping them means allowing something worse to fester in the dark. Besides, Null already knows I have it. They knew when they asked. The only question was whether I valued my secrecy more than the potential to understand what we’re truly up against.”

They spent the next few hours meticulously preparing the data packet for Null. Alex triple-scrubbed the Kilo-Core logs for any personal identifiers, a nerve-wracking task. Eve compiled her Project Brother notes – fragmented code snippets, old design documents she’d managed to squirrel away, her own theoretical analyses. It was a digital ghost of a project, a testament to ambition and oversight.

With a shared sense of trepidation, they sent the package to Null.

The confirmation was, as always, terse.
`> Exchange packet received. Verification in progress. Stand by for our data.`

The silence that followed was different this time. It wasn't just the absence of information; it was the silence of a transaction made, a pact sealed in the shadowy corners of the internet. They had paid the price. Now, they waited to see if the whispers they’d bought would illuminate their path, or lead them deeper into the darkness. Alex couldn’t shake the feeling that by inviting Null into their confidence, they had gained a powerful, unpredictable ally, but also potentially, an equally dangerous observer.
