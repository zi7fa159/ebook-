# Writing Your First SCP: Tips and Guidance for Aspiring Foundation Authors

## 1. Introduction: Joining the Ranks of Anomaly Creators

The SCP Foundation universe is a vast and captivating collaborative fiction project, and the idea of contributing your own unique anomaly to its ever-expanding database is an exciting prospect. However, writing a successful SCP article that resonates with the community and meets the site's quality standards can be a challenging endeavor, especially for newcomers. The SCP Wiki has a distinct style, a discerning readership, and a robust critique process.

This article aims to provide aspiring Foundation authors with essential tips, guidance, and resources to help them navigate the process of conceptualizing, drafting, critiquing, and ultimately (hopefully) posting their first successful SCP article. Welcome to the anomalous world of creative writing!

## 2. Before You Write: Immerse Yourself in the Universe

The single most important step before attempting to write an SCP is to become deeply familiar with the existing material.
*   **Read Widely:** Don't just read the most famous SCPs (the "Heritage Collection" or top-rated). Explore different series (Series I through VIII as of this writing), read tales, delve into GoI formats, and check out different canons. This will give you a feel for:
    *   The range of possible anomalies (not just monsters!).
    *   The clinical tone and scientific formatting.
    *   Common tropes (and how to subvert or avoid them).
    *   The kind of storytelling that resonates with the community.
*   **Understand Object Classes:** Know the difference between Safe, Euclid, Keter, Thaumiel, Apollyon, and Neutralized/Explained. Understand that classification is about *containment difficulty*, not necessarily direct danger.
*   **Learn Basic Lore:** Familiarize yourself with key concepts like the O5 Council, D-Class personnel, Mobile Task Forces, The Veil, amnestics, and major Groups of Interest. You don't need to be an expert, but a working knowledge helps.
*   **Study Successful Articles:** Analyze SCPs you enjoy. What makes them effective? Is it the core concept, the narrative in the addenda, the psychological horror, the clever containment procedures? Deconstruct what works.
*   **Check "The Deletions Log":** The SCP Wiki has a section for recently deleted articles. Reading why articles failed can be as instructive as reading why they succeeded. Common pitfalls include clichés, poor execution, or lack of an interesting hook.

## 3. Conceptualization: Finding Your Anomaly

Every SCP starts with an idea. Here's how to develop a strong one:
*   **Originality is Key (But Execution Matters More):** While a truly unique idea is great, even a familiar concept can shine with excellent execution, a fresh twist, or compelling narrative. Avoid direct copies of existing SCPs.
*   **The "Hook":** What makes your anomaly interesting? What is its core mystery, horror, or unique property? An SCP needs a hook to grab the reader's attention. It could be:
    *   A terrifying monster.
    *   A bizarre object with strange effects.
    *   A compelling human story tied to an anomaly.
    *   A conceptual or philosophical puzzle.
    *   A clever subversion of expectations.
*   **More Than Just a "Thing That Kills You":** While dangerous SCPs are common, an anomaly that *only* kills people in a straightforward way is often considered uninteresting. Think about *how* it's dangerous, *why* it's dangerous, or what the *implications* of its danger are.
*   **Narrative Potential:** A good SCP often has a story embedded within it, even if told through clinical procedures and addenda. Think about:
    *   Its discovery.
    *   Incidents involving it.
    *   Research findings.
    *   The human element (how it affects personnel, victims, or even itself if sentient).
*   **Consider the "Why":** Why does this anomaly exist (in-universe)? While not always necessary to explain, having a hint of a backstory or an unknown purpose can add depth.
*   **Avoid Common Pitfalls:**
    *   **Generic Monsters:** "It's a big scary monster that kills everyone" is usually too simple.
    *   **Overpowered Anomalies (Mary Sues/Gary Stus):** Entities that are invincible, omniscient, and can do anything are often boring. Limitations and vulnerabilities make anomalies more interesting.
    *   **"Cool" Factor Over Substance:** An idea might seem "cool" but lack narrative depth or a compelling hook.
    *   **Forcing Crosslinks:** Don't try to shoehorn in references to famous SCPs (like 682 or 173) unless it genuinely serves your story.

## 4. Mastering the Format and Clinical Tone

The SCP format is crucial to its appeal and believability.
*   **Standard Sections:**
    *   **Item #:** Your SCP's designation (e.g., SCP-XXXX).
    *   **Object Class:** Safe, Euclid, Keter, etc. Justify your choice.
    *   **Special Containment Procedures:** This is where you detail *how* the Foundation contains the anomaly. Be specific, logical (within the bounds of the anomaly), and consider resource implications. Good procedures enhance realism.
    *   **Description:** Clearly and clinically describe what the anomaly is, its appearance, and its anomalous properties. Avoid overly flowery or emotional language here.
    *   **Addenda (Optional but often crucial):** This is where much of the storytelling happens – interview logs, experiment logs, incident reports, recovered documents, historical notes. Use these to reveal more about the anomaly, its history, or its effects.
*   **Clinical Tone:**
    *   **Objective and Scientific:** Write like a scientist or a government agent filing a technical report. Use precise language.
    *   **Detached:** Avoid overly emotional or subjective descriptions, especially in the main Description and Procedures. The horror often comes from the *contrast* between the detached tone and the horrific events being described.
    *   **Formal Language:** Use formal vocabulary and sentence structures.
    *   **[REDACTED] and [DATA EXPUNGED]:** Use these sparingly and purposefully. They are used to imply information that is too sensitive, too dangerous (infohazardous), or simply unknown. Overuse makes an article look lazy or like it's hiding a lack of ideas. They should enhance mystery, not replace content.
*   **Pacing and Revelation:** Don't reveal everything at once. Build suspense and intrigue. Use addenda to gradually unfold the story or the full extent of the anomaly's properties.

## 5. The Drafting Process: Getting It Down

*   **Sandbox First:** The SCP Wiki has a "Sandbox" site where you can create drafts of your articles privately or share them for feedback without posting to the main list. **Always use the Sandbox first.** Do not post unfinished or un-critiqued work directly to the main site.
*   **Outline Your Idea:** Before writing the full article, create an outline of the core concept, containment procedures, description, and any narrative elements you want to include in addenda.
*   **Iterate:** Your first draft will rarely be your best. Be prepared to revise, rewrite, and cut material based on feedback.
*   **Show, Don't Just Tell:** Instead of saying an anomaly is "terrifying," describe its effects or the reactions of those who encounter it in a way that *makes* the reader feel terror. Experiment logs and incident reports are great for this.

## 6. The Critique Process: Essential for Improvement

Critique is a vital and mandatory part of the SCP writing process, especially for new authors.
*   **Seek Feedback:**
    *   **SCP Wiki Forums:** There are dedicated forums for seeking critique on drafts.
    *   **IRC Chatrooms / Discord Servers:** Real-time chatrooms are excellent for getting quick feedback and brainstorming. (The main SCP Wiki site lists official chat venues).
    *   **Be Specific:** When asking for critique, you can ask for general feedback or focus on specific areas you're unsure about (e.g., "Is the clinical tone right?" "Are the containment procedures plausible?").
*   **Receiving Critique:**
    *   **Be Open-Minded:** Critiques are meant to help you improve. Don't take negative feedback personally.
    *   **Listen to Multiple Opinions:** Different people will have different perspectives. Look for common themes in the feedback.
    *   **Don't Argue (Usually):** If someone misunderstands something, it might mean your writing isn't clear enough. Thank them for their time and consider how to clarify.
    *   **You Don't Have to Take All Advice:** Ultimately, it's your article, but ignore consistent, strong advice at your peril.
*   **Giving Critique:** Participating in critiquing others' work is a great way to learn and become a better writer yourself. It helps you understand what makes SCP articles work (or not work).

**Do not post your article to the main list until you have received substantial positive feedback and "greenlights" (if required by current site policy for new authors).**

## 7. Posting and Beyond

*   **Follow Site Rules:** Read the "How to Write an SCP" guide and other site policy documents carefully. They cover formatting, image use, tagging, and posting procedures.
*   **Tagging:** Correctly tag your article with relevant keywords (e.g., "humanoid," "memetic," "keter," "object") so readers can find it.
*   **Author Post (Optional):** After posting, you can create an author post in the article's discussion page to thank critters, offer insights, or clarify non-obvious elements (but avoid over-explaining your SCP – let it speak for itself).
*   **Handling Votes:**
    *   Your article will be upvoted or downvoted by the community. Don't obsess over the numbers immediately.
    *   If your article receives significant downvotes and falls below the deletion threshold (usually -10), it will be deleted by staff. This is normal and happens to many authors, even experienced ones. Learn from the experience, get more critique, and try again.
*   **Engage with Comments (Optional):** You can respond to comments in your article's discussion page, but maintain a professional and respectful demeanor.

## 8. Final Tips for Success

*   **Read, Read, Read:** The best way to learn is to read what's successful and what's not.
*   **Find Your Niche:** What kind of horror or sci-fi interests you? Write what you're passionate about.
*   **Don't Be Afraid to Be Weird:** The SCP universe thrives on unique, strange ideas.
*   **Focus on a Single Core Idea:** Don't try to cram too many anomalous properties into one SCP. Simplicity and focus are often more effective.
*   **Story is King:** Even the most clinical SCP article is telling a story. Make sure yours is compelling.
*   **Be Patient:** Writing a good SCP takes time, effort, and multiple revisions.
*   **Have Fun!** At its heart, this is a creative writing project. Enjoy the process of creation and collaboration.

Writing for the SCP Wiki can be an incredibly rewarding experience, allowing you to contribute to a unique and globally recognized fictional universe. Good luck, and may your anomalies be anomalous in all the right ways!
