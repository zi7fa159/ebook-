# Euclid-Class SCPs: The Unpredictable Norm of the Anomalous World

## 1. Introduction: The Realm of Uncertainty

In the SCP Foundation's meticulous system for categorizing the anomalous, the Euclid class occupies a vast and often perplexing middle ground. More demanding to contain than Safe-class objects but generally less resource-intensive or actively hostile than Keter-class entities, Euclid SCPs represent the "unknowns" that require active monitoring and tailored procedures. An SCP is classified as Euclid if it is not sufficiently understood, if its behavior is unpredictable, or if it is autonomous and not entirely controllable, yet doesn't pose an immediate, overwhelming threat that demands Keter-level resources.

The Euclid classification is, in many ways, the default for new or enigmatic anomalies. It signifies that while the Foundation has a grasp on containment, there are variables at play that prevent a "lock it and forget it" approach. This article will explore the nuances of the Euclid class, its defining characteristics, common types of Euclid SCPs, and why it represents the everyday challenge of the Foundation's mission.

## 2. Defining "Euclid": The Box Test and Beyond

The "Box Test" provides a simple starting point:
*   **Euclid:** If you lock it in a box, leave it alone, and you're not quite sure what will happen, or it requires specific ongoing actions to keep it from doing something unexpected, it's probably Euclid.

This uncertainty is the hallmark of the Euclid class.

**Key Criteria for Euclid Classification:**
1.  **Incomplete Understanding:** The Foundation may not fully comprehend the anomaly's properties, capabilities, triggers, or long-term behavior. Research is often ongoing.
2.  **Autonomous or Sentient Nature:** Many Euclid SCPs are living entities, sentient beings, or autonomous objects that can act independently, making their behavior less predictable than inert Safe-class items.
3.  **Specific Containment Needs:** Containment procedures are often more complex than for Safe SCPs, requiring regular maintenance, specific environmental conditions, provision of resources (like food for living SCPs), or defined interaction protocols.
4.  **Potential for Escalation:** While not actively seeking to breach containment like many Keters, a Euclid SCP might become hostile or dangerous if its needs are not met, if it's improperly handled, or due to unknown triggers.
5.  **Reliable Containment Requires Vigilance:** Containment is generally achievable, but it demands consistent attention and adherence to specialized procedures. A lapse could lead to a breach or dangerous incident.

Essentially, a Euclid SCP is one that "needs watching" more carefully than a Safe one, but isn't trying to end the world every Tuesday like a Keter.

## 3. Common Characteristics and Types of Euclid SCPs

The Euclid class is incredibly diverse, encompassing a wide range of anomalous phenomena:

*   **Autonomous Entities:**
    *   **Creatures/Beings:** Many anomalous creatures that are not overtly hostile but require specific habitats, feeding schedules, or whose behavior isn't fully predictable fall into this class. (e.g., SCP-173 "The Sculpture" – its behavior is understood, but requires constant active observation).
    *   **Sentient Humanoids:** Humanoid SCPs that are cooperative to a degree but possess anomalous abilities or whose loyalty cannot be absolutely guaranteed are often Euclid. (e.g., SCP-049 "The Plague Doctor" – cooperative but driven by its own inscrutable agenda).
*   **Objects with Unpredictable Effects:**
    *   Items whose anomalous properties activate inconsistently, are triggered by unknown variables, or have effects that vary over time.
    *   Anomalies that react to their environment or observers in complex ways not fully mapped out.
*   **Location-Based Anomalies:**
    *   Buildings or areas with localized anomalous properties that are stable as long as certain protocols are followed (e.g., no one enters a specific room, specific rituals are performed). If these protocols are simple and reliable, it might be Safe; if they are complex or the effects of failure are uncertain, it leans Euclid.
*   **Self-Replicating or Self-Altering Objects:**
    *   Anomalies that can reproduce or change their form/properties over time, requiring monitoring to manage their population or new characteristics.
*   **Cognitohazards or Memetic Agents:**
    *   Information-based anomalies whose spread is somewhat controllable but whose effects can be dangerous if exposure occurs. If the information is easily contained (e.g., a single document locked away), it might be Safe. If it requires active counter-memetics or its spread is harder to control, it's often Euclid. (e.g., SCP-096 "The Shy Guy" is Euclid because its trigger is specific, though the consequences are Keter-level).
*   **Newly Discovered Anomalies:** Most new SCPs are initially classified as Euclid until they are sufficiently understood to be reclassified as Safe (if simple to contain) or Keter (if proving exceptionally dangerous and difficult).

The common thread is a degree of agency or unpredictability that necessitates more than passive containment.

## 4. Examples of Euclid-Class SCPs

The Euclid class is the most populous in the SCP database. Here are a few illustrative examples:

*   **SCP-173 ("The Sculpture"):**
    *   **Why Euclid:** Requires constant, direct observation (an active measure). Failure to observe results in immediate, lethal action. It's autonomous. While its rule is simple, the containment is demanding.

*   **SCP-049 ("The Plague Doctor"):**
    *   **Why Euclid:** Generally cooperative but possesses a lethal touch and an unpredictable urge to "cure" individuals, turning them into SCP-049-2 instances. Its motivations are not fully understood.

*   **SCP-096 ("The Shy Guy"):**
    *   **Why Euclid:** Its trigger (viewing its face) is specific. If its face is never seen, it remains docile. However, the consequence of this trigger is a catastrophic, unstoppable rampage, making any potential exposure point a severe risk. The containment challenge is ensuring absolute visual secrecy.

*   **SCP-087 ("The Stairwell"):**
    *   **Description:** An unlit platform staircase that seems to descend endlessly. Contains SCP-087-1 (a mysterious face) and distressing auditory phenomena.
    *   **Why Euclid:** The anomaly is geographically fixed and doesn't actively try to expand. However, exploration is dangerous, its full extent is unknown, and its effects on those who descend are not fully understood. Containment involves securing the entrance and preventing unauthorized access.

*   **SCP-1609 ("The Remains of a Chair"):**
    *   **Description:** A pile of wood splinters, nails, and fabric scraps, formerly a teleporting chair that became aggressive after being damaged by the Global Occult Coalition. It teleports into the lungs of individuals wearing GOC-style uniforms or acting aggressively nearby.
    *   **Why Euclid:** It's inert unless specific trigger conditions (aggression, GOC uniforms) are met. However, these triggers can be common in a Foundation environment if not carefully managed. Its reaction is hostile and dangerous.

*   **SCP-3008 ("A Perfectly Normal, Regular Old IKEA"):**
    *   **Description:** An IKEA retail store where the interior retail space is effectively infinite and labyrinthine, trapping customers. Contains a rudimentary civilization of trapped customers (SCP-3008-1) and hostile "Staff" entities (SCP-3008-2) that appear at night.
    *   **Why Euclid:** The entrance is a single point. Containment involves monitoring this entrance and preventing entry/exit. However, the internal dimension is vast, poorly understood, and actively dangerous.

These examples show the breadth of the Euclid class – from single entities to vast extradimensional spaces.

## 5. Containment Challenges: Balancing Act

Containing Euclid SCPs is a constant balancing act for the Foundation:
*   **Resource Allocation:** They require more resources than Safe SCPs (e.g., dedicated personnel, specialized cells, ongoing research) but generally less than the constant high-alert state needed for Keters.
*   **Research Priority:** Understanding a Euclid SCP better is often a high priority, as this understanding could lead to reclassification as Safe (making containment cheaper and easier) or confirm a need for Keter-level protocols.
*   **Personnel Risk:** Interacting with Euclid SCPs – for research, feeding, or cell maintenance – often carries a higher risk to personnel than with Safe items. Procedures must be meticulously followed.
*   **Unforeseen Interactions:** Euclid SCPs, due to their unpredictable nature, have a higher chance of unexpected interactions with other anomalies or environmental factors, potentially leading to breaches or new dangerous phenomena.

## 6. Why Euclid is the "Default" or Most Common Class

Many within the SCP community consider Euclid to be the "default" classification for several reasons:
*   **The Nature of the Anomalous:** Anomalies, by definition, defy easy understanding. It's rare for a newly discovered phenomenon to be immediately understood well enough for Safe classification or to be so overtly and uncontrollably hostile as to warrant an immediate Keter tag.
*   **Cautionary Principle:** The Foundation operates with a high degree of caution. Until an anomaly's behavior is thoroughly documented and its containment proven reliable over time, classifying it as Euclid ensures it receives the necessary level of attention and resources.
*   **Ongoing Discovery:** The SCP universe is one of constant discovery. Many anomalies are encountered with incomplete information, making Euclid the most appropriate initial assessment.

This doesn't mean all SCPs *stay* Euclid, but it's a common starting point and a stable classification for many that remain somewhat enigmatic or require specific, ongoing care.

## 7. The Psychological Impact: Living with Uncertainty

For Foundation personnel, Euclid-class anomalies represent the everyday reality of their work. Unlike the sheer terror a Keter might inspire or the deceptive calm of a Safe object, Euclids embody a persistent, low-grade tension.
*   **Constant Vigilance:** Personnel know that these anomalies require careful handling and that procedures must be followed to the letter, as a lapse could have serious consequences.
*   **The "What If" Factor:** The inherent unpredictability means there's always a "what if" factor. What if it reacts differently today? What if we've missed a trigger?
*   **Normalization of the Strange:** Ironically, the sheer number of Euclid SCPs can lead to a normalization of strangeness for experienced staff, though this can also breed complacency if not carefully managed.

## 8. Conclusion: The Heartbeat of the Foundation's Work

The Euclid class is far more than just a miscellaneous category; it is the beating heart of the SCP Foundation's daily operations and challenges. These anomalies, with their blend of partial understanding and inherent unpredictability, demand constant vigilance, careful research, and tailored containment strategies. They represent the vast majority of what the Foundation wrestles with – phenomena that are too complex or autonomous for simple storage, yet not so overwhelmingly chaotic as to require the extreme measures reserved for Keter-class threats.

Understanding the Euclid classification is key to understanding the core mission of the Foundation: to navigate a world filled with uncertainties, to impose order where little exists, and to protect humanity from threats that often defy easy categorization but always demand respect and careful management. Every Euclid SCP is a puzzle being solved, a risk being managed, and a testament to the Foundation's ongoing struggle to Secure, Contain, and Protect in a universe that consistently refuses to play by the rules.
