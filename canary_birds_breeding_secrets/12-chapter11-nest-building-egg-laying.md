# Chapter 11: Nest Building and Egg Laying - Ensuring Success

Once a compatible canary pair has settled together and mating has likely occurred, the next exciting phase is nest building, closely followed by the arrival of the first precious egg. This period requires continued excellent care and minimal disturbance to ensure the hen feels secure and can successfully complete these natural processes. This chapter focuses on supporting your canaries through nest construction and the egg-laying period, offering secrets to maximize success.

## 11.1. The Nest Building Process

For a hen canary, the drive to build a nest is a powerful instinct when she is in breeding condition.

*   **The Hen Takes the Lead:** While the cock bird might occasionally show interest or even carry a piece of material, the hen is almost exclusively responsible for constructing the nest.
*   **Material Selection:** She will meticulously select strands of nesting material you provide, carrying them to the chosen nest site.
*   **Weaving and Shaping:** Using her beak and body, she weaves the materials together, gradually forming a cup shape. She will frequently sit in the forming nest, rotating her body to press and mold it into the perfect size and contour. This process can take several days.
*   **A Finished Nest:** A well-built nest is typically a neat, compact cup, tightly woven and deep enough to securely hold the eggs and later, the chicks.

### 11.1.1. Providing the Right Nest and Materials:

*   **Nest Types (Recap):**
    *   **External Nests:** Plastic or wire, hung on the outside of the cage. Easy to inspect.
    *   **Internal Nests:** Wicker, plastic, or pottery, placed inside.
    *   Ensure the nest is clean and securely fastened to prevent wobbling.
*   **Nest Liners (Optional but Recommended):**
    *   Clean, commercial felt or paper nest liners can provide a smooth base, help the nest keep its shape, and can be easily replaced if soiled between clutches.
*   **Nesting Materials – The "Secrets" to Good Choices:**
    *   **Variety:** Offer a couple of different types of material if possible. Some hens have preferences.
    *   **Ideal Materials:**
        *   **Commercial Canary Nesting Material:** Usually a blend of cotton, jute, sisal, or animal hair (e.g., sanitized horsehair). These are generally safe and effective.
        *   **Coconut Fiber:** Natural, durable, and well-liked by many canaries.
        *   **Untreated Burlap/Hessian:** Cut into short strips (2-3 inches). Ensure it's clean and untreated.
        *   **Short Pieces of Soft String/Yarn (Natural Fibers Only):** Cotton or wool, cut into very short pieces (1-2 inches maximum). **CRITICAL: Avoid long strands that can entangle birds.**
        *   **Dried, Soft Grasses (Pesticide-Free):** If you can source clean, soft, dried grasses, these can be used.
    *   **Materials to AVOID:**
        *   **Long Cotton Threads/Strings:** High risk of entanglement for legs, wings, or necks of adults or chicks.
        *   **Synthetic Fibers (Nylon, Polyester):** Don't breathe well, can be slippery, and long strands are dangerous.
        *   **Human Hair:** Can easily tangle.
        *   **Fine, Dusty Materials:** Can cause respiratory issues.
    *   **Quantity:** Provide a generous amount of material, more than she will likely use. Replenish as needed.
    *   **Presentation:** Place nesting material in a specific holder (commercial or a clean spring) or loosely on the cage floor in a clean area. Don't stuff it directly into the nest pan – let her do the work.

### 11.1.2. The Cock Bird's Role During Nest Building:

*   A well-behaved cock bird will generally sing to the hen, perform courtship displays, and may feed her while she is building.
*   He should not interfere with her work. If he persistently pulls the nest apart or tries to take over, he might need to be separated by a divider, especially once eggs are laid.

## 11.2. The Egg Laying Process

*   **Timing:** Typically, a hen will lay her first egg 5-10 days after successful mating and intensive nest building.
*   **Time of Day:** Eggs are almost always laid in the early morning, often just after dawn.
*   **One Egg Per Day:** A hen will lay one egg per day until her clutch is complete.
*   **Clutch Size:** The average clutch size for canaries is 3-6 eggs. First-time hens may lay smaller clutches.
*   **Egg Appearance:** Canary eggs are small, typically pale blue or greenish-blue, often with reddish-brown or lavender speckles, especially around the larger end. Color and speckling can vary.

### 11.2.1. "Dummy" Eggs and Synchronized Hatching (A Breeder's Secret):

*   **Why Synchronize Hatching?** Canaries typically begin serious incubation after the second or third egg is laid. This means the first eggs laid will hatch earlier, resulting in chicks of different sizes in the same nest. Larger, older chicks can outcompete smaller, younger ones for food, sometimes leading to the loss of the youngest chicks.
*   **The Technique:**
    1.  **Remove Freshly Laid Eggs:** Each morning, carefully remove the newly laid egg.
    2.  **Replace with "Dummy" Eggs:** Replace the real egg with a plastic or wooden dummy egg (available from bird supply stores). This makes the hen feel like her clutch is growing and encourages her to continue laying.
    3.  **Store Real Eggs:** Store the collected real eggs at room temperature (around 55-65°F or 13-18°C), pointed end down, in a container with some soft seed or bran to cushion them. Turn them once or twice a day to prevent the yolk from sticking.
    4.  **Return Real Eggs:** Once the hen has laid her third or fourth egg (or her entire clutch, if you prefer), remove all the dummy eggs and return all the collected real eggs to the nest at the same time, usually in the evening.
*   **Benefit:** This ensures all eggs begin incubation simultaneously, leading to all chicks hatching within a 24-36 hour period. This gives all chicks a more equal chance of survival.
*   **Caution:** Handle real eggs very gently with clean hands or a soft plastic spoon. If you are not comfortable with this technique, letting the hen incubate naturally is also acceptable, but be prepared for staggered hatching.

### 11.2.2. Supporting the Hen During Egg Laying:

*   **Nutrition:** Continue providing high-quality egg food/soft food daily. Calcium (cuttlebone, crushed oyster shell) is absolutely critical for shell formation.
*   **Minimal Disturbance:** This is paramount. Avoid loud noises, sudden movements near the cage, or unnecessary handling of the hen or nest. Stress can cause a hen to abandon her nest or stop laying.
*   **Water for Bathing:** Some hens appreciate a bath during egg laying; it can help with humidity and feather condition.
*   **Observation:** Check for new eggs each morning discreetly.

## 11.3. Common Issues During Nest Building and Egg Laying

*   **Hen Not Building a Nest:**
    *   **Not in Condition:** She may not be fully ready. Review conditioning protocols.
    *   **Wrong Nesting Material:** Try offering different types.
    *   **Insecure Nest Site:** Ensure the nest is stable and in a somewhat sheltered location in the cage.
    *   **Male Interference:** Is the male bothering her?
*   **Poorly Built Nest:** Some young or inexperienced hens build flimsy nests. You can try to gently reinforce it or replace it with a pre-formed felt liner once she starts laying.
*   **Eggs Laid on Cage Floor:**
    *   **Nest Not Ready/Accepted:** She may not like the nest provided or wasn't ready when the urge to lay struck.
    *   **First-Time Hen:** Sometimes inexperienced hens do this.
    *   **Action:** Gently place the egg in the nest. If she continues, ensure the nest is acceptable and she feels secure.
*   **Egg Binding (Dystocia):**
    *   **Signs:** Hen appears distressed, fluffed up, straining, sitting on the cage floor, possibly with a swollen abdomen. This is a serious emergency.
    *   **Causes:** Calcium deficiency, oversized egg, young or old hen, cold temperatures, obesity.
    *   **Action (Immediate):** Provide warmth (e.g., move cage to a warmer room, use a heat lamp carefully). Offer a drop of liquid calcium orally if possible. Place a few drops of warm vegetable oil around her vent. If the egg is not passed within an hour or two, **seek immediate avian veterinary attention.** Egg binding can be fatal.
*   **Soft-Shelled or Shell-less Eggs:** Usually due to calcium deficiency. Ensure constant access to multiple calcium sources.
*   **Hen Eating Her Own Eggs:**
    *   **Causes:** Often a sign of calcium deficiency, stress, boredom, or a bad habit.
    *   **Action:** Ensure high calcium availability. Remove eggs promptly (if using dummy eggs). Sometimes providing a decoy (like a dummy egg) can satisfy the urge. If it persists, the hen may not be suitable for breeding.

## Chapter Summary

The nest building and egg-laying phase is a testament to the canary's natural instincts. Success hinges on providing a secure environment, appropriate nesting materials, and excellent nutrition, especially calcium for the hen. The technique of using dummy eggs to synchronize hatching is a valuable breeder's secret for improving chick survival rates. Minimizing disturbances and being vigilant for potential problems like egg binding are crucial. With careful support, your hen will likely present you with a clutch of eggs, marking the next step towards new canary life.
