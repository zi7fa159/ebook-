# Chapter 10: Pairing Your Canaries - Signs of a Good Match

Once your canaries are properly conditioned and showing clear signs of breeding readiness, the exciting step of formally pairing them begins. Successful pairing is not just about putting a male and a female together; it's about observing their interactions, ensuring compatibility, and creating an environment conducive to mating. This chapter covers the process of introducing your conditioned pair and recognizing the signs that indicate a good, promising match.

## 10.1. Recap: Are They Both Ready?

Before physically introducing the pair for breeding, quickly review the signs of peak breeding condition discussed in Chapter 9:

*   **Cock Bird (Male):**
    *   Persistent, loud, and complex song.
    *   Restless, active, and "displaying" (dropped wings, dancing).
    *   Prominent cloacal protuberance (swollen, pointed vent).
    *   May attempt to feed the hen through a divider if one was used.

*   **Hen Bird (Female):**
    *   Increased chirping, responding to the male's song.
    *   Actively gathering and arranging nesting material.
    *   Crouching and soliciting the male (quivering wings/tail).
    *   Vent area may appear broader and slightly swollen.

**Crucial Point:** Both birds should exhibit these signs. Attempting to pair birds where one is significantly less conditioned than the other often leads to problems like aggression, disinterest, or infertile eggs. Patience in waiting for both to be ready is key.

## 10.2. The Introduction Process

There are a few methods for introducing a pair. The gradual approach is generally recommended to minimize stress and aggression.

### 10.2.1. Gradual Introduction (Using a Divider):

This method was touched upon in conditioning but is formalized here for pairing.
1.  **Initial Setup:** Place the selected cock and hen in the breeding cage, separated by a wire divider that allows them to see and hear each other but not make full physical contact. Ensure each side has food and water.
2.  **Observation Period (Several Days to a Week):**
    *   **Male's Behavior:** He should sing intensely towards the hen. He might try to pass food to her through the divider wires – this is an excellent sign of bonding.
    *   **Hen's Behavior:** She should appear interested in the male, possibly chirping back, and may start showing nesting behaviors on her side of the cage if nesting material is available. She might approach the divider when he is near.
    *   **Aggression Check:** Note any signs of excessive aggression from either bird towards the divider. Some initial chasing or assertiveness from the male is normal, but persistent attempts to attack are a concern.
3.  **Removing the Divider:**
    *   Once both birds seem interested and compatible through the divider, and the hen is actively trying to build a nest or solicit, it's time to remove the divider.
    *   **Timing:** It's often best to remove the divider in the morning so you can observe them for several hours.
    *   **Immediate Observation:** Stay nearby (but not intrusively) for the first hour or two to monitor their interactions.

### 10.2.2. Direct Introduction (Use with Caution):

Some breeders directly introduce conditioned birds into the breeding cage together.
*   **When it Might Work:** If both birds are clearly in peak condition and you know them to be of compatible temperaments (perhaps they've bred successfully before).
*   **Risks:** Higher chance of initial aggression, especially from the male if the hen is not immediately receptive, or from a territorial hen.
*   **If Attempting:** Ensure the cage is spacious with multiple perches and escape routes. Be prepared to separate them immediately if serious fighting occurs.

## 10.3. Signs of a Good Match: Positive Interactions

Once the divider is removed or the birds are directly introduced, look for these positive signs indicating a compatible pair:

*   **Mutual Interest:** Both birds seem aware of and interested in each other, rather than ignoring one another.
*   **Male Courting, Hen Receptive:**
    *   The cock bird will intensify his courtship song and displays (dancing, wing dropping) directed at the hen.
    *   The hen may respond by crouching low on the perch, quivering her wings and tail, and making soft chirps. This is the classic solicitation posture inviting copulation.
*   **Courtship Feeding (Allopreening/Allogrooming):**
    *   The male may offer regurgitated food to the female. This is a very strong bonding behavior and a clear sign of a good match. The hen will typically flutter her wings and accept the food.
    *   They might also gently preen each other's head and neck feathers.
*   **Shared Nest Building (Primarily Hen, Male May Assist):**
    *   The hen will take the lead in constructing the nest.
    *   A well-matched male might show interest, occasionally bringing her a piece of nesting material or sitting near her while she builds. He should not disrupt her efforts.
*   **Tolerance and Lack of Serious Aggression:**
    *   Minor squabbles or the male chasing the hen a bit initially can be normal as they establish dominance or test receptivity.
    *   However, this should quickly settle down. Persistent, vicious attacks (feather plucking, pinning the other bird down) are **not** a sign of a good match, and the birds should be separated.
*   **Attempted or Successful Copulation:**
    *   If the hen solicits, the male will attempt to mount her. Successful copulation is very brief (a few seconds) and involves the "cloacal kiss." You may see several mating attempts throughout the day.

## 10.4. Signs of a Poor Match or Incompatibility

Be vigilant for signs that the pairing is not working out:

*   **Persistent, Severe Aggression:**
    *   One bird (often the male, but sometimes the hen) constantly attacks the other, plucking feathers, or preventing access to food/water or the nest.
    *   **Action:** Separate immediately. Try reintroducing with a divider or consider a different mate.
*   **Complete Indifference:** The birds ignore each other entirely, even after several days together. The male doesn't sing to the hen, or the hen shows no interest in him or nesting.
    *   **Action:** Check if both are truly in condition. If so, they may simply be incompatible.
*   **Hen Fearful of Male:** The hen constantly tries to hide from the male, appears stressed, and does not solicit.
    *   **Action:** The male might be too aggressive, or the hen not yet fully conditioned or simply not interested in that particular male. Separation and re-evaluation are needed.
*   **Male Disrupts Nest Building:** The male persistently interferes with the hen's attempts to build the nest, throws material out, or tries to sit in the empty nest himself excessively.
    *   **Action:** This can sometimes be managed by temporarily separating the male with a divider once egg-laying begins, allowing him to still feed the hen.
*   **No Mating Attempts Despite Solicitation:** If the hen solicits but the male makes no attempt to mate, he may not be in full condition, be too old, too young, or have other issues.

## 10.5. What to Do If a Pair Doesn't "Click"

*   **Re-Check Condition:** Are both birds truly at their peak? Sometimes a little more time on conditioning diet and light is needed.
*   **Try the Divider Again:** If separated due to aggression, reintroduce with a divider for a longer period.
*   **Swap Mates (If You Have Options):** If you have other conditioned birds available, sometimes a different partner is all that's needed. Canary "preferences" can be a factor!
*   **Give Them a Break:** If a pair consistently fails to bond or is aggressive, separate them for the season and try again next year, or consider them unsuitable as a pair. Forcing an incompatible pair is stressful and unproductive.

## 10.6. The First Few Days Together

Once a pair seems compatible and is housed together without a divider:
*   **Provide Nest and Materials:** Ensure a clean nest and ample nesting material are available.
*   **Continue Excellent Nutrition:** Daily egg food, fresh greens, and their quality seed mix.
*   **Minimize Disturbances:** This is a critical time. Avoid unnecessary cage movements, loud noises, or excessive peering into the cage.
*   **Observe Discreetly:** Monitor for continued positive interactions, nest building progress, and eventually, egg laying.

## Chapter Summary

Successfully pairing canaries involves more than just placing two birds together; it requires careful observation of their readiness and interactions. Gradual introduction using a divider is often the safest method. Key signs of a good match include mutual interest, the male's dedicated courtship displays, the hen's receptive solicitation, courtship feeding, and cooperative (or at least tolerant) behavior around nest building. Conversely, persistent aggression, complete indifference, or fear are indicators of incompatibility. Being patient, observant, and willing to separate or re-pair birds if necessary are crucial skills for the breeder during this delicate phase.
