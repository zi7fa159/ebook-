# Chapter 12: Incubation Secrets - Monitoring and Assisting

Once the hen has laid her complete clutch of eggs and begins to sit tightly, the incubation period starts. This is a time of quiet anticipation for the breeder, but also one where careful, non-intrusive monitoring and understanding the hen's needs are crucial. While canaries are generally good incubators, knowing a few "secrets" and how to assist if needed can make the difference between a successful hatch and disappointment.

## 12.1. The Incubation Process: What's Happening?

*   **Who Incubates?** Primarily the hen. The cock bird typically does not sit on the eggs, though he may feed the hen on the nest.
*   **Start of Incubation:** Most hens begin serious, consistent incubation after the second or third egg is laid, or once the clutch is complete (especially if you've managed egg collection for synchronized hatching).
*   **Incubation Period:** The average incubation period for canary eggs is **13-14 days** from the day serious incubation begins. This can vary slightly based on ambient temperature and the hen's diligence.
*   **Temperature and Humidity:** The hen's body provides the warmth (around 99.5°F or 37.5°C at egg level) and humidity necessary for embryonic development. She also turns the eggs regularly to ensure even heating and prevent the embryo from sticking to the shell membrane.
*   **Hen's Behavior:** A dedicated incubating hen will sit tightly on the nest, usually only leaving for very brief periods to eat, drink, and defecate. She will fluff her feathers to cover the eggs effectively.

## 12.2. Monitoring the Incubating Hen and Nest

Minimal disturbance is key during incubation, but discreet monitoring is important.

*   **Daily Checks (Non-Intrusive):**
    *   **Is the Hen Sitting?** Observe from a distance. Is she consistently on the nest?
    *   **Food and Water:** Ensure she has easy access to fresh food and water. The cock bird may be feeding her, but she should also be able to access resources herself if needed.
    *   **Hen's Condition:** Does she look healthy when she briefly leaves the nest? Alert and active, not fluffed up or lethargic (which could signal illness or egg-binding if she's still laying).
    *   **Cage Cleanliness:** Maintain basic cage hygiene by changing bottom paper regularly, but avoid deep cleaning or rearranging the cage in a way that would disturb her.

*   **"Candling" Eggs (A Breeder's Secret for Fertility Check):**
    *   **What is Candling?** Candling involves shining a bright light through the egg to observe embryonic development. It allows you to determine if eggs are fertile and developing, or if they are clear (infertile) or have died early.
    *   **When to Candle:** The best time is around **day 5-7 of incubation**. By this time, a fertile egg will show a network of fine blood vessels (like spider veins) and often a small, dark spot which is the developing embryo. Clear eggs will just show the yolk shadow and remain translucent.
    *   **How to Candle:**
        1.  Use a small, bright LED candler or a small flashlight with its beam narrowed by a piece of cardboard with a small hole.
        2.  Work in a darkened room for best visibility.
        3.  Gently and quickly remove one egg at a time from the nest when the hen is off (e.g., feeding). Handle with very clean hands or a plastic spoon.
        4.  Hold the egg up to the candler. Do not overheat the egg.
        5.  Return the egg promptly to the nest.
    *   **Interpreting Candling Results:**
        *   **Fertile:** Visible blood vessels, embryo.
        *   **Infertile (Clear):** No vascular development, only yolk visible.
        *   **Early Death (Blood Ring):** A distinct red or dark ring inside the shell indicates the embryo started developing but died.
    *   **Benefits of Candling:**
        *   Allows removal of clear eggs or dead embryos, preventing them from potentially rotting and contaminating viable eggs.
        *   Gives an early indication of the success of the pairing.
        *   If all eggs are clear, you can re-pair the birds sooner rather than waiting the full incubation period.
    *   **Caution:** Handle eggs minimally and with extreme care. If you are unsure or uncomfortable, it's okay to skip candling and let nature take its course. Excessive handling or chilling can harm developing embryos.

## 12.3. Supporting the Hen During Incubation

*   **Consistent Diet:** Provide her regular high-quality seed mix, fresh water, and continue offering egg food/soft food. While she may eat less, having nutritious food available is important for when she does take breaks. The cock bird will also need good nutrition if he is feeding her.
*   **Calcium:** Keep cuttlebone available.
*   **Bathing:** Offer a bath every 2-3 days. Some hens will use it, which can help maintain humidity around the eggs. Remove it after a couple ofhours.
*   **Peace and Quiet:** This is the most crucial support. Avoid loud noises, sudden movements, other pets near the cage, or frequent inspections that might cause her to abandon the nest.
*   **The Cock Bird's Role:**
    *   A good male will often feed the incubating hen on the nest. This allows her to sit tighter and leave the eggs less frequently.
    *   If the male is aggressive towards the hen on the nest or tries to drive her off, he should be separated by a cage divider. He can still feed her through the wires.

## 12.4. Common Incubation Problems and Solutions

*   **Hen Not Sitting Tight/Abandoning Nest:**
    *   **Causes:** Too young/inexperienced, excessive disturbance, infertile eggs (some hens seem to know), illness, mites or parasites in the nest, incompatible male.
    *   **Action:** Minimize all disturbances. Check for mites (tiny red or grey specks, often visible at night). Ensure the environment is secure. If she persists in abandoning, the clutch may be lost. Consider fostering eggs if you have another incubating hen (see section 12.5).
*   **Clear (Infertile) Eggs:**
    *   **Causes:** Immature birds, out-of-condition pair, incompatible pair, sterile male or female (rare), nutritional deficiencies, stress during mating.
    *   **Action:** If candling confirms all eggs are clear, you can remove them and allow the pair to try again after a rest and re-conditioning. Review your conditioning and pairing process.
*   **Dead in Shell (DIS):**
    *   **Causes:** Bacterial or fungal contamination, poor parental nutrition, genetic issues, incorrect incubation temperature/humidity (less common with natural incubation if the hen is diligent), prolonged chilling if hen stays off nest too long.
    *   **Action:** Review hygiene, diet, and minimize disturbances. Ensure good ventilation in the bird room.
*   **Eggs Broken in Nest:**
    *   **Causes:** Thin-shelled eggs (calcium deficiency), clumsy hen, overly active male, overcrowded nest.
    *   **Action:** Remove broken eggs and soiled nesting material immediately to prevent contamination. Ensure adequate calcium. If the male is the cause, consider separating him with a divider.
*   **Hen Lays More Eggs After Starting Incubation:** Sometimes a hen will lay an additional egg or two after she has seemingly started incubating a clutch. This will lead to very staggered hatching.

## 12.5. Fostering Eggs (An Advanced "Secret")

If a hen abandons her nest, or if you have a particularly valuable clutch of eggs from a problematic hen, it's sometimes possible to foster the eggs under another broody canary hen (or even a related species like a society finch, though canaries are best).

*   **Requirements for a Foster Hen:**
    *   Must be in breeding condition and already incubating her own clutch (or dummy eggs) reliably.
    *   Ideally, she should be at a similar stage of incubation or have just started.
*   **Process:**
    *   Carefully and quickly transfer the desired eggs to the foster hen's nest, preferably when she is off feeding.
    *   It's best to add them to an existing clutch if she has one, rather than giving her only fostered eggs, though some hens will accept a full new clutch.
    *   Do not overload the foster nest (max 5-6 eggs total is usually manageable).
*   **Risks:** The foster hen may reject the new eggs or abandon her own. This is a technique best used when the original clutch is otherwise certain to be lost.

## 12.6. Preparing for Hatch Day

As the expected hatch date (around day 13-14) approaches:
*   **Increase Humidity Slightly (Optional):** Some breeders lightly mist the area around the cage (not directly on the nest) or ensure a bath is available more frequently in the day or two before hatching. This can help prevent chicks from getting stuck in the shell if the membrane is too dry.
*   **Prepare Soft Food:** Ensure you have high-quality egg food/soft food ready, as this will be the primary food for the newly hatched chicks, fed to them by the parents.
*   **Anticipation!** Resist the urge to constantly check the nest. Let the hen do her job.

## Chapter Summary

The incubation period is a quiet but critical phase. The hen provides the necessary warmth, humidity, and turning for the eggs. The breeder's role is to provide a stable, stress-free environment, excellent nutrition for the hen (and cock, if he's feeding her), and to monitor discreetly. Candling around day 5-7 can help identify infertile eggs, but should be done with care. Understanding common incubation problems and having strategies like fostering (if necessary and feasible) are valuable "secrets" for the dedicated breeder. As hatch day nears, ensuring high humidity and having soft food ready will prepare you for the arrival of new chicks.
