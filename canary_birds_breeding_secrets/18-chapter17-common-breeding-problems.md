# Chapter 17: Dealing with Common Breeding Problems

Even with the best preparation and care, canary breeders will inevitably encounter problems. From infertile eggs to troublesome parents, these challenges can be frustrating. However, understanding the common issues, their potential causes, and effective solutions can help you navigate these hurdles and improve your breeding success. This chapter addresses some of the most frequent breeding problems.

## 17.1. Infertile Eggs (Clear Eggs)

One of the most common disappointments is finding a clutch of clear eggs after candling or waiting the full incubation period.

*   **Potential Causes:**
    *   **Immature Birds:** Cock or hen not yet sexually mature (typically need to be 9-12 months).
    *   **Old Birds:** Reduced fertility in birds over 4-5 years.
    *   **Poor Condition:** Either or both birds not in peak breeding condition (insufficient light, poor diet).
    *   **Lack of Successful Mating:**
        *   Incompatible pair (no bonding, aggression).
        *   Male not actively courting or mating.
        *   Hen not receptive or soliciting.
        *   Physical interference (e.g., overly dense vent feathers, unstable perches).
    *   **Sterility:** One of the birds may be sterile (rare, but possible).
    *   **Stress:** Excessive disturbances during the mating period.
    *   **Obesity:** Overweight birds are often less fertile.
    *   **Nutritional Deficiencies:** Lack of Vitamin E or other key nutrients.
    *   **Male Overuse:** If a cock bird is paired with too many hens without rest.

*   **Solutions & Prevention:**
    *   **Verify Age and Condition:** Ensure birds are of appropriate age and in peak health/condition (Chapter 9).
    *   **Observe Mating:** Try to confirm successful copulation.
    *   **Re-evaluate Pairing:** If incompatible, consider a different mate. Use a divider for gradual introduction.
    *   **Check Perches:** Ensure they are stable.
    *   **Vent Feather Trimming (Caution):** Consider as a last resort if dense feathers seem to be an issue (Chapter 16).
    *   **Optimize Diet and Lighting:** Review your conditioning protocols.
    *   **Rest Males:** Don't overwork your cock birds.
    *   **Record Keeping:** Track which pairs produce clear eggs to identify potential individual fertility issues.
    *   **Patience:** Sometimes a young pair needs a "practice round."

## 17.2. Dead in Shell (DIS)

Eggs are fertile and develop for a period but die before hatching.

*   **Potential Causes:**
    *   **Bacterial/Fungal Infections:** Contamination of the eggshell or nest.
    *   **Nutritional Deficiencies in Hen:** Lack of essential vitamins or minerals passed to the embryo.
    *   **Genetic Abnormalities:** Lethal genes or developmental issues.
    *   **Incorrect Incubation:**
        *   Temperature too high or too low (less common with natural incubation if hen is diligent).
        *   Insufficient humidity (chick can get "stuck" in dry membranes).
        *   Hen not sitting tightly or leaving the nest for extended periods.
    *   **Rough Handling of Eggs:** During candling or nest checks.
    *   **Weak Embryos:** From unhealthy or poorly conditioned parents.
    *   **Pesticide/Toxin Exposure.**

*   **Solutions & Prevention:**
    *   **Hygiene:** Strict nest and cage hygiene. Clean nesting material for each round.
    *   **Parental Nutrition:** Ensure a superior diet for parent birds before and during breeding.
    *   **Minimize Egg Handling:** Be gentle if candling.
    *   **Consistent Incubation:** Minimize disturbances to the incubating hen.
    *   **Humidity:** Provide bathing opportunities for the hen, or lightly mist the room (not the nest) if air is very dry, especially near hatch time.
    *   **Avoid Inbreeding Closely:** Increases risk of genetic issues.
    *   **Good Ventilation:** In the bird room to prevent stale air and mold.

## 17.3. Egg Binding (Dystocia)

The hen is unable to pass an egg. This is a serious emergency.

*   **Signs:** Hen appears distressed, fluffed up, straining, sitting on cage floor, weakness, rapid breathing, swollen abdomen.
*   **Potential Causes:**
    *   **Calcium Deficiency:** Most common cause, leading to weak muscle contractions and/or soft-shelled eggs.
    *   **First-Time Layer/Young Hen:** Immature reproductive system.
    *   **Old Hen:** Weakened muscles.
    *   **Oversized or Malformed Egg.**
    *   **Obesity or Poor Muscle Tone.**
    *   **Cold Temperatures/Drafts.**
    *   **Stress or Disturbance During Laying.**

*   **Immediate Action (See Chapter 11 for more detail):**
    *   **Warmth:** Provide immediate warmth (85-90°F / 29-32°C). A hospital cage or placing a heat lamp carefully near one end of the cage.
    *   **Humidity:** Increase humidity (e.g., place cage in a bathroom with a hot shower running for steam, or use a humidifier).
    *   **Calcium:** Offer liquid calcium (e.g., a drop of calcium glubionate orally or on soft food if she'll take it).
    *   **Lubrication:** A tiny drop of vegetable oil or KY jelly carefully applied to the vent area *might* help if the egg is visible.
    *   **Minimize Stress.**
    *   **VETERINARY EMERGENCY:** If the egg is not passed within 1-2 hours of these measures, **seek immediate avian veterinary assistance.** Manual removal or hormonal injections may be needed. Delay can be fatal.

*   **Prevention:**
    *   **Abundant Calcium:** Always available (cuttlebone, oyster shell, crushed eggshell).
    *   **Proper Conditioning Diet.**
    *   **Avoid Breeding Very Young or Very Old Hens.**
    *   **Maintain Healthy Weight.**
    *   **Stable, Warm Environment.**

## 17.4. Abandoned Nest or Chicks

Parents stop caring for eggs or chicks.

*   **Potential Causes:**
    *   **Excessive Disturbance:** Loud noises, frequent cage movements, too much inspection by the breeder.
    *   **Inexperienced Parents (First-timers).**
    *   **Stress:** Presence of predators (even household pets), night frights.
    *   **Illness in Parent(s).**
    *   **Infertile Eggs:** Some hens abandon clutches they sense are not viable.
    *   **Lack of Bonding with Chicks:** Less common, but can occur.
    *   **Mites or Parasites:** Heavy infestation in the nest can drive parents away.
    *   **Desire to Start a New Nest:** Sometimes a hen is eager to lay another clutch and may abandon older chicks.

*   **Solutions & Prevention:**
    *   **Minimize All Disturbances:** Crucial during incubation and early chick rearing.
    *   **Provide a Secure Environment.**
    *   **Monitor Parental Health.**
    *   **Control Mites.**
    *   **Fostering Eggs/Chicks:** If abandonment occurs, fostering under another suitable pair is an option (Chapter 12 & 14).
    *   **Hand-Feeding:** For abandoned chicks, this is a life-saving last resort (Chapter 14).
    *   **Remove Male (If Problematic):** If the male is harassing the hen or chicks, separate him with a divider.

## 17.5. Parents Not Feeding Chicks

*   **Potential Causes:**
    *   **Inexperienced Parents:** Especially first-time parents, may not immediately understand feeding cues.
    *   **Stress or Disturbance.**
    *   **Inappropriate or Unpalatable Soft Food:** If they don't like the egg food/soft food provided, they won't feed it.
    *   **Illness in Parents or Chicks.**
    *   **Chicks Too Weak to Gape:** If chicks are chilled or ill, they may not stimulate parents to feed.
    *   **Male Not Assisting:** Sometimes the hen does all the work initially.

*   **Solutions & Prevention:**
    *   **Offer Highly Palatable Soft Food:** Ensure fresh, appealing egg food is always available. Try different types or recipes if they are picky.
    *   **Minimize Disturbances.**
    *   **Check Chick Health:** Ensure chicks are warm and gaping.
    *   **Supplemental Hand-Feeding:** If chicks are not being fed adequately, temporary supplemental hand-feeding may be needed to keep them strong until parents take over.
    *   **Patience with New Parents:** Sometimes they take a day or so to get the hang of it. Monitor crop fullness.

## 17.6. Aggression Between Paired Birds

*   **Male Aggression Towards Hen:**
    *   **Causes:** Male in much stronger condition than hen, hen not receptive, territoriality, incompatible personalities.
    *   **Action:** Separate with a divider. Allow visual contact and reintroduce when hen is clearly soliciting. If aggression persists, the pair may be unsuitable.
*   **Hen Aggression Towards Male:**
    *   **Causes:** Hen very territorial of nest area, male overly persistent or clumsy, hen not truly in condition.
    *   **Action:** Usually less severe, but if persistent, a divider may be needed, especially after egg laying begins.
*   **Aggression Towards Chicks:**
    *   **Causes:** Stress, parents wanting to start a new nest (male may attack fledglings), overcrowding.
    *   **Action:** Separate the aggressive parent immediately. If it's the male and chicks are young, the hen can often raise them alone. If chicks are older/fledging, move them to a nursery cage.

## 17.7. Feather Plucking (Parents Plucking Chicks)

*   **Causes:**
    *   **Hen Wants to Re-Nest:** Most common cause. She plucks feathers from older chicks to line a new nest.
    *   **Nutritional Deficiencies (Rarely):** Lack of certain proteins or minerals.
    *   **Boredom/Stress.**
    *   **Overcrowding.**

*   **Solutions & Prevention:**
    *   **Provide New Nesting Material:** If the hen is plucking to re-nest, immediately offer fresh nesting material in a new nest pan or different location in the cage. This often diverts her attention.
    *   **Separate Male:** If the hen is determined to re-nest quickly, sometimes removing the male (after chicks are a bit older and she's feeding well) can calm her focus on the current clutch.
    *   **Ensure Balanced Diet.**
    *   **Move Fledglings:** If chicks are old enough and being plucked, move them to a separate weaning cage.
    *   **Anti-Pluck Sprays (Use with Caution):** Bitter-tasting sprays for birds can deter plucking, but address the underlying cause first. Ensure they are safe for chicks.

## Chapter Summary

Breeding canaries comes with its share of potential problems, from infertile eggs and dead-in-shell embryos to egg binding, abandonment, and aggression. Understanding the likely causes of these common issues is the first step toward effective prevention and management. Key strategies often revolve around ensuring optimal parental health and condition, providing excellent nutrition, minimizing stress and disturbances, maintaining scrupulous hygiene, and careful observation. While not all problems can be avoided, a knowledgeable and proactive breeder can significantly improve their chances of navigating these challenges successfully. Always be prepared to consult an avian veterinarian for serious health concerns.
